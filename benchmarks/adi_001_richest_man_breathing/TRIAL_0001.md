# ADI Benchmark 001 — Trial 0001

## Status

This is the first small-horizon generation checkpoint built from the benchmark's decompressed intent anchors. It is **not** evidence for long-horizon cognitive amplification. Its purpose is to establish the narrative-state format and test whether generation follows the story's dependency structure instead of jumping directly to later spectacle.

# Chapter Two — Respiratory Economics

Ren Fujitsu woke up with his forehead pressed against the steering wheel.

This was alarming for several reasons.

The first was that he had not remembered putting his forehead there.

The second was that his vision had acquired little black stars around the edges.

The third was that his banking app was still open in his right hand.

AVAILABLE BALANCE: $61.17

Ren stared at it.

Then he stared at the steering wheel.

Then, very carefully, he inhaled through his nose.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

He exhaled.

One.

Two.

Three.

BZZT.

+$1.00

Ren closed his eyes.

"Good news," he whispered. "I'm rich."

He swallowed.

"Bad news. I'm an idiot."

The parking lot outside Walmart had continued existing during his brief attempt to discover whether hypoxia could be monetized. Cars moved past. A woman returned a shopping cart. Somewhere nearby, a child screamed with the sustained commitment of someone who had recently learned that public spaces had acoustics.

Ren leaned back in the driver's seat and breathed normally for a while.

Not profitably.

Just normally.

This turned out to be much harder than expected.

He had spent twenty-six years breathing without once considering himself responsible for it. Now every inhale felt like a financial decision.

Too short.

Too long.

Was that exhale longer?

Had he just lost a dollar?

His phone did not buzz.

Ren frowned.

"Okay. We need rules."

He opened the notes app.

At the top of a new page, he typed:

MONEY BREATHING TESTS

Then beneath it:

1. Stop almost killing yourself.

He considered this.

"Strong start."

He added:

2. Confirm negative condition.
3. Confirm equal condition.
4. Find safe sustainable rhythm.
5. Figure out if the IRS can tax oxygen.

He stared at number five.

That one felt less like a joke the longer he looked at his bank balance.

Ren set a stopwatch on his phone.

"Negative first."

He inhaled for three seconds.

Then exhaled for four.

The phone vibrated.

BANK ADJUSTMENT: -$1.00

AVAILABLE BALANCE: $60.17

Ren made a wounded noise.

It was an objectively ridiculous response to losing one dollar after materializing eighteen of them out of nowhere, but poverty had trained certain reflexes deeply.

"I hated that."

He typed:

2. Confirmed. Losing money feels worse when you personally breathed it away.

Next test.

Four seconds in.

Four seconds out.

Nothing.

He waited.

Still nothing.

He refreshed the bank app.

$60.17.

"Equal is neutral."

He wrote it down.

Then he stopped.

The first genuinely useful thought of the afternoon finally arrived.

Why was he trying to maximize breaths?

Ren looked at the numbers he'd written earlier.

Ten profitable cycles per minute was six hundred dollars an hour.

Six hundred.

An hour.

He had spent forty-one minutes making $6.82.

His brain had apparently adapted to supernatural income in less than fifteen minutes and was already furious that six hundred dollars per hour wasn't enough.

"Capitalism has damaged me psychologically."

He deleted a half-finished note titled FASTEST SAFE RESPIRATION RATE HUMAN.

Then he opened the stopwatch again.

He tried five seconds in, four seconds out.

Easy.

Again.

Easy.

Again.

After six cycles he felt a little too conscious of the process, but not dizzy.

Six dollars in fifty-four seconds.

He tried four in, three out.

Ten cycles.

No problem.

Ten dollars in seventy seconds.

Then three in, two out.

By the fifteenth cycle his chest began asking why management had suddenly introduced performance metrics.

Ren stopped.

"Right."

He added another rule.

6. The lungs are employees, not slaves.

He sat there for a moment, letting his breathing return to normal.

His phone buzzed twice during that time.

Ren froze.

He hadn't been counting.

He looked at the notifications.

+$1.00

+$1.00

His eyebrows rose.

He inhaled naturally.

Then paid attention to the exhale.

His ordinary resting breath was close enough that sometimes the inhale happened to be slightly longer.

Not always.

But sometimes.

"Oh."

That changed the optimization problem completely.

He didn't need to spend his life doing breathing exercises like a deranged yoga accountant.

He needed a comfortable rhythm that could become automatic.

Ren adjusted the driver's seat, put the phone on the center console, and spent the next twenty minutes doing something he would have mocked mercilessly that morning.

He practiced breathing.

Four seconds in.

Three seconds out.

Pause only when comfortable.

Again.

He tried walking around the car while maintaining it.

That worked.

He tried opening the trunk.

Worked.

He tried checking the passenger-side tire.

The rattling noise did not reveal itself through visual inspection, which meant his longstanding diagnostic strategy remained technically undefeated.

Another dollar appeared.

He walked into Walmart.

The automatic doors opened.

Ren inhaled.

Four.

Exhaled.

Three.

+$1.00

He grabbed a cart.

Another breath.

+$1.00

For years, entering a grocery store had started an involuntary calculation in his head.

How much could he spend?

What could wait?

What was cheaper somewhere else?

Could he stretch what was at home another day?

He reached the produce section and looked at a container of strawberries.

$4.98.

Ren stopped walking.

Five breaths.

That was all.

Five breaths.

He stood there staring at the strawberries with an emotion wildly disproportionate to fruit.

The first thing infinite money bought him was not a sports car.

It was the ability to put strawberries in a cart without checking whether bananas were cheaper per calorie.

Ren picked them up.

Then immediately put them back.

"No."

He took a different container.

The first one had a moldy strawberry at the bottom.

Supernatural income had not yet made him stupid.

He bought groceries.

Not luxury groceries.

He discovered there was apparently an income level at which his preferences did not instantly transform into imported cheese and steaks.

Chicken.

Rice.

Eggs.

Vegetables.

Cereal.

Coffee.

The strawberries.

Then, after standing in front of the frozen section for nearly a minute, he added the expensive ice cream.

He felt rebellious doing it.

At self-checkout, the total came to $67.43.

Ren stared at the screen.

His balance had climbed to $173.17 during his experiments and shopping.

He paid.

AVAILABLE BALANCE: $105.74

Ren waited.

Inhaled.

Exhaled.

+$1.00

$106.74.

He laughed quietly.

The woman at the next register glanced at him.

Ren pretended the bananas were hilarious.

Outside, he loaded the groceries into the Hyundai and sat behind the wheel again.

His delivery app was still closed.

That bothered him.

Not because he wanted to work.

Because part of him felt like he was doing something wrong.

Three years of gig work had conditioned him to experience inactivity as a financial emergency.

If the car wasn't moving, money wasn't happening.

If he declined an order, he was losing potential income.

If he went home early, that choice had a number attached to it.

But now—

Ren inhaled.

Four seconds.

Exhaled.

Three.

+$1.00

Now sitting still was technically a revenue stream.

He leaned his head back against the seat.

"I don't have to work tonight."

The sentence felt illegal.

He said it again.

"I don't have to work tonight."

Nothing happened.

No bill appeared to punish him.

No app lowered his acceptance rate.

No invisible supervisor materialized to explain hustle culture.

His phone buzzed.

+$1.00

Ren looked at the grocery bags beside him.

Then at the fuel gauge.

He still needed gas.

The old version of the calculation arrived automatically.

Twenty dollars?

Maybe thirty.

Enough to get through tomorrow.

Ren caught himself.

He drove to the gas station and pulled beside a pump.

For several seconds he simply stared at the buttons.

Regular.

Price per gallon.

The usual little decisions.

Then he inserted his card.

"Fill."

The pump clicked on.

Ren watched the numbers rise.

Ten dollars.

Twenty.

Thirty.

For once, he didn't squeeze the handle early.

He let the tank fill completely.

The pump stopped.

$48.12.

Ren looked at the receipt.

Something tight in his chest loosened.

Not dramatically.

There was no music.

No notification announcing a realm breakthrough.

He had simply purchased gasoline without first deciding which other problem would remain unsolved because of it.

His phone buzzed.

+$1.00

Ren smiled.

Then his expression changed.

"Wait."

He opened his notes.

At the bottom he typed:

7. Tonight: test whether sleeping counts.

He stared at the words.

If it did—

Ren glanced at the time.

2:47 PM.

Less than an hour ago, $6.82 had been forty-one minutes of his life.

Now the most financially consequential thing he could possibly do might be taking a nap.

Ren started the car.

The passenger-side wheel rattled ominously.

He listened to it for three seconds.

Then, for the first time since buying the Hyundai, he did not turn the radio up.

"Actually," Ren said, pulling out his phone, "we're fixing that tomorrow."

He inhaled.

Four.

Exhaled.

Three.

+$1.00

And drove home.

### Semantic verdict

**PASS for local intent alignment.** The chapter advances the same story promised by the seed without consuming later mysteries or skipping prerequisite development.

This does **not** validate the ADI theory. At two chapters the dependency burden is trivial. The value of the trial is that the state/obligation ledger is now explicit enough to carry forward and later be tested for drift.

### New obligations created

1. Chapter 3 must test sleep profitability.
2. The car repair should occur soon enough that Ren's first uses of money remain grounded and causally consistent.
3. Ren's earnings need an explicit tracked timeline so Day 4's compliance freeze is quantitatively earned.
4. His breathing rhythm should gradually become easier before Perfect Respiration, but not become magically automatic before the 10,000-cycle milestone.
5. The story should begin distinguishing "money problem solved" from "life/problem-solving competence solved"—Ren can now buy room to think, but he is not yet powerful or knowledgeable.
